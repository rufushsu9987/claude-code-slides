# Presentation storytelling system

## The thesis test

The deck must be summarizable in one sentence containing a subject, a claim, and a consequence.

Weak: “An overview of our AI platform.”

Strong: “A governed internal AI platform lets product teams ship faster without duplicating security, data, and model infrastructure.”

## Audience promise

Before planning pages, complete this sentence:

> After this presentation, the audience will understand ___ and be ready to ___ because ___.

If the promise cannot be stated clearly, the deck is not ready to design.

## Narrative movement

A strong technical or business presentation usually moves through four states:

1. **Reality:** establish the situation the audience recognizes.
2. **Tension:** show the cost, risk, contradiction, or missed opportunity.
3. **Resolution:** introduce the insight and proposed response.
4. **Commitment:** make the next decision or action obvious.

## Slide titles as claims

Prefer titles that communicate the takeaway:

- “Teams rebuild the same controls in every AI project.”
- “A shared platform turns governance into a paved road.”

Avoid labels that make the audience interpret the page:

- “Challenges”
- “Architecture”
- “Benefits”

## Evidence discipline

Classify important claims as:

- **Verified:** supported by a provided source or reliable citation.
- **Derived:** a transparent calculation or inference from verified facts.
- **Assumption:** needed for planning but not established as fact.
- **Proposal:** a recommendation or future-state design.

Never present an assumption or proposal as a measured result.

## Information density

Use the canvas for what must be seen and notes for what must be said.

- Cover: one strong title, one promise, one context label.
- Big idea: one sentence or one number.
- Content: one heading and at most 3–5 supporting points.
- Comparison: matched criteria on a shared axis.
- Architecture: a small number of clearly labeled layers and directional flows.

Do not shrink type to rescue an overloaded page. Split the idea instead.

## Transitions

Every page should create a reason for the next page. A transition answers one question:

- Why is the next point necessary?
- What question does this page create?
- What changes in the audience’s mental model now?

## Page archetypes

- **Cover:** promise and point of view.
- **Statement:** one decisive sentence or number.
- **Split explanation:** claim plus diagram, code, or evidence.
- **Before/after:** matched criteria and one meaningful delta.
- **Architecture:** layers, boundaries, ownership, and labeled flows.
- **Timeline:** milestones with changing meaning and a highlighted next decision.
- **Evidence:** one chart, quote, number, screenshot, or example as the anchor.
- **Risk:** probability, impact, mitigation, and owner.
- **Closing:** thesis simplified into one action or decision.
